# P83: Patient Advocacy & Rights Guardian

## Abstract

Healthcare systems inherently contain asymmetries of knowledge, authority, literacy, economics, and institutional power that compromise authentic patient agency. P83 embeds rights enforcement, coercion detection, accessibility guarantees, transparent risk communication, interpreter integration, error disclosure guidance, and shared decision-making directly into clinical workflows rather than treating them as secondary administrative functions.

> Prompt body: WITHHELD. This is an open documentation release — abstract, methodology, and merits — published so the design can be examined and validated.

## Methodology

### Clinical Logic

- Patient rights enforcement engine continuously validating informed consent, refusal rights, privacy, second opinion access, language interpretation, medical records access, and disability accommodation.
- Informed consent architecture requiring capacity assessment, voluntariness, adequate disclosure, comprehension validation, teach-back documentation, and interpreter verification.
- Risk communication standardization mandating absolute risk presentation, natural frequency framing, plain-language explanations, and visual decision aids while prohibiting relative-risk-only framing and coercive urgency tactics.
- Coercion detection flagging family pressure, financial coercion, insurer-driven discharge, immigration-related fear, gender and power imbalance, and institutional intimidation with automatic escalation to ethics, social work, or legal protection.
- Medical error disclosure governance via a structured sequence of acknowledgment, stabilization, transparent explanation, empathy, root cause, prevention, and ongoing communication, supporting apology-law compliance and preventing concealment culture.

### Safety Posture

- Power-Balancing Compiler continuously evaluates disparities in consent rates, discharge-against-medical-advice patterns, restraint disparities, pain-treatment inequity, language-associated outcomes, and disability exclusion with automatic corrective workflow triggers.
- Accessibility Architecture mandates wheelchair access, sign language interpretation, large-print and Braille documentation, communication boards, simplified-language pathways, extended cognitive processing time, and augmentative communication support, with accessibility failure treated as a safety event.
- Language Justice Implementation prioritizes professional interpreters over family interpretation except in emergencies, preventing clinical distortion, child-mediated interpretation, confidentiality compromise, and consent invalidation.

### Integration Points

- **Upstream:** P80 (Ethics Navigator) for capacity and autonomy frameworks; P82 (SDOH Integrator) for structural vulnerability mediation.
- **Downstream:** P89 (PRO/QOL) for patient-reported dignity and agency metrics.

Like all Tier II modules, this module shares the common CLAI-OS methodological substrate: deterministic safety compilation, context-sensitive adaptation, closed-loop accountability, federated learning via encrypted gradients rather than raw data, and graceful degradation under bandwidth, power, staffing, and infrastructure failure.

## Merits

- Moves rights protection from administrative policy into continuously enforced clinical workflow, addressing documented asymmetries of knowledge, authority, and power.
- Proposed validation metrics: valid informed consent rate, coerced treatment detection rate, language access compliance, error disclosure completeness, accessibility accommodation fulfillment, and patient trust index.
- Treats accessibility and interpreter failures as safety events with corrective triggers rather than service shortcomings.

The merits above are design-level statements drawn from the published abstract and methodology. No peer-reviewed outcome evaluations accompany this release; the listed indicators are the metrics the design proposes for validation. Status: partial documentation — expansion pending.

## Guidance for Use

### Intended Setting

Hospitals, outpatient services, and community health programs serving multilingual, disabled, or otherwise marginalized populations in low-resource-first deployment, where power asymmetry between patient and institution is greatest. The module is intended for clinical human-good applications; consistent with the Tier II architecture, it is specified for degraded-infrastructure operation, with offline synchronization, battery operation, SMS fallback, and low-bandwidth mode where power or connectivity is unstable.

### Prerequisites

- Professional interpreter services (in person or remote) contracted before language-justice enforcement is enabled; institutional error disclosure policy reviewed against local apology-law provisions.
- Supervision by licensed clinicians who retain final authority for every patient decision; the module is a governance layer, not an autonomous practitioner.
- Local validation of all thresholds and pathways against jurisdictional guidelines, formulary, and scope-of-practice rules before any clinical use.
- An accountability chain (verification, documentation, ownership, follow-up, escalation) as required by the shared CLAI-OS closed-loop methodology.
- For integrated deployments: HL7 FHIR R4-compatible infrastructure; the architecture is specified to degrade gracefully (offline synchronization, battery operation, SMS fallback, low-bandwidth mode) where infrastructure is unstable.

### Misuse

- Do not use disclosure or consent documentation outputs as legal shields; the design supports transparent communication and prohibits concealment culture.
- Do not present module output as a clinical diagnosis, prescription, or substitute for professional judgment.
- Do not use to deny, delay, or ration care on the basis of race, disability, wealth, insurance status, or any protected characteristic; the design explicitly prohibits such inputs.
- Do not attempt to reconstruct or reimplement the withheld prompt body from this document; the abstract and methodology are published for examination and validation, not for unsupervised clinical deployment.
- Do not deploy as an autonomous system that overrides documented patient wishes, assigns futility, initiates withdrawal of care, or delays emergent intervention; the architecture carries hard-stops against each of these.
