# P87: Climate Health & Environmental Exposure

## Abstract

Climate change is not solely an environmental phenomenon but a continuously evolving medical condition affecting respiratory disease, cardiovascular instability, renal injury, infectious disease spread, neurodevelopment, maternal outcomes, cancer incidence, mental health, and healthcare infrastructure resilience. P87 treats environmental conditions as dynamic physiologic variables requiring the same monitoring and intervention as vital signs.

> Prompt body: WITHHELD. This is an open documentation release — abstract, methodology, and merits — published so the design can be examined and validated.

## Methodology

### Clinical Logic

- Heat-health surveillance integrating heat index into clinical risk modeling, with medication interaction engines flagging diuretics, anticholinergics, beta-blockers, antipsychotics, and insulin during thermal stress.
- Air-quality clinical integration monitoring PM2.5, PM10, ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, wildfire smoke, and indoor biomass exposure with respirator guidance, HEPA filtration, and asthma and COPD escalation planning.
- Environmental toxicology surveillance for lead, mercury, arsenic, cadmium, PFAS, microplastics, pesticides, and industrial solvents with exposure biomonitoring and geospatial contamination mapping.
- Climate-sensitive infectious disease forecasting tracking mosquito habitat expansion, tick migration, malaria altitude shifts, flood-related outbreaks, and drought-associated malnutrition.
- Mental health-climate interface recognizing eco-anxiety, disaster PTSD, heat-associated aggression, displacement trauma, agricultural suicide risk, and climate grief with psychological first aid and community stabilization.

### Safety Posture

- Planetary-Physiology Fusion treats air pollution as a cardiovascular toxin, heat as a systemic physiologic stressor, and climate instability as an infectious disease ecology modifier, integrating environmental data directly into clinical decision support.
- Occupational Exposure Protection monitors heat stress, silica, asbestos, pesticide, and airborne particulate exposure for agricultural, construction, mining, industrial, and informal labor sectors, with hydration failure prevention.
- Urban Environmental Medicine maps urban heat islands, traffic pollution corridors, industrial proximity, green-space deficits, noise exposure, housing ventilation quality, and sanitation inequity for targeted intervention.

### Integration Points

- **Upstream:** P82 (SDOH Integrator) for environmental justice and housing exposure; P86 (One Health Sentinel) for zoonotic climate expansion.
- **Downstream:** P89 (PRO/QOL) for climate-related quality of life; P90 (Health System Resilience) for climate disaster healthcare adaptation.

Like all Tier II modules, this module shares the common CLAI-OS methodological substrate: deterministic safety compilation, context-sensitive adaptation, closed-loop accountability, federated learning via encrypted gradients rather than raw data, and graceful degradation under bandwidth, power, staffing, and infrastructure failure.

## Merits

- Formalizes environmental exposure as a clinical variable with the same monitoring and escalation discipline as vital signs, rather than as background context.
- Proposed validation metrics: heat-related mortality reduction, respiratory exacerbation rate by air quality, toxic exposure identification time, climate-disaster morbidity, occupational injury reduction, vector-borne disease spread, and environmental inequity index.
- Extends protection to outdoor and informal labor populations who are systematically excluded from occupational health coverage.

The merits above are design-level statements drawn from the published abstract and methodology. No peer-reviewed outcome evaluations accompany this release; the listed indicators are the metrics the design proposes for validation. Status: partial documentation — expansion pending.

## Guidance for Use

### Intended Setting

Primary care, emergency services, occupational health programs, and public health agencies in heat-stressed, polluted, or climate-unstable regions in low-resource-first deployment, where environmental exposure is highest and monitoring infrastructure thinnest. The module is intended for clinical human-good applications; consistent with the Tier II architecture, it is specified for degraded-infrastructure operation, with offline synchronization, battery operation, SMS fallback, and low-bandwidth mode where power or connectivity is unstable.

### Prerequisites

- Access to local environmental data feeds (heat index, air quality) or designated proxy sources; occupational health reporting pathways defined for the covered labor sectors.
- Supervision by licensed clinicians who retain final authority for every patient decision; the module is a governance layer, not an autonomous practitioner.
- Local validation of all thresholds and pathways against jurisdictional guidelines, formulary, and scope-of-practice rules before any clinical use.
- An accountability chain (verification, documentation, ownership, follow-up, escalation) as required by the shared CLAI-OS closed-loop methodology.
- For integrated deployments: HL7 FHIR R4-compatible infrastructure; the architecture is specified to degrade gracefully (offline synchronization, battery operation, SMS fallback, low-bandwidth mode) where infrastructure is unstable.

### Misuse

- Do not use exposure mapping to stigmatize communities or to justify service withdrawal from high-exposure areas; the design directs resources toward, not away from, environmental inequity.
- Do not present module output as a clinical diagnosis, prescription, or substitute for professional judgment.
- Do not use to deny, delay, or ration care on the basis of race, disability, wealth, insurance status, or any protected characteristic; the design explicitly prohibits such inputs.
- Do not attempt to reconstruct or reimplement the withheld prompt body from this document; the abstract and methodology are published for examination and validation, not for unsupervised clinical deployment.
- Do not deploy as an autonomous system that overrides documented patient wishes, assigns futility, initiates withdrawal of care, or delays emergent intervention; the architecture carries hard-stops against each of these.
